# Iteration-Method Notes — how the mock-driven session produced the best results

*Source: the CTRL strategy/design session (transcript `405c3265-…jsonl`) + `_DESIGN-LOG.md`. These feed the `BUILD-PARTNER-PLAYBOOK.md`. Every observation below is grounded in something that actually happened in the session; the founder is quoted verbatim where quoted.*

The founder named the method himself and asked us to capture it: *"I want to document the entirety of this journey, particularly for mindmaker live learnings and also as a bluprint for how you and I build apps together."* Here is what made it work.

1. **One mock, then PAUSE — never fire several ahead.** The cadence was: build ONE HTML mock → open it in a browser → the founder reacts → lock the decision → next surface. The single most important rule. When the agent skipped the check-in, the founder caught it immediately: *"Hold on, you didnt ask if I was happy with the last mock up."* Locking the cadence (not the agent racing ahead) is what kept every decision deliberate and owned.

2. **React to a RENDERED artifact, not a description.** Decisions were made against a real browser render the founder could see and feel, not against prose. This is why his visual-OCD eye could fire at all — you cannot catch *"the word 'contested' pushes the text out of alignment"* or *"the visualization falls off the right-hand edge of the card"* by reading a spec. The mock is the unit of truth; the words about the mock are not.

3. **The founder's visual-OCD eye catches cramming the spec never would.** A running stream of precise, render-only defects drove the whole quality climb: *"a lot of text truncates, which is absolutely unacceptable… 'stack or build our own' wraps poorly"*; *"the title at the top of the page still looks word doc-like, and has one word wrap to the next line"*; *"the word 'week' wraps onto a new line… and then the visualization falls off the right-hand edge."* Each tiny break, taken seriously, exposed a SYSTEMIC fault — not a one-frame patch.

4. **Lock the decision before moving on — and write it down.** Every reaction produced a `LOCKED -` line in `_DESIGN-LOG.md` before the next surface started. Nothing was re-opened later. The founder's own framing: separate DESIGN (the agreed shape — frozen, never re-touched) from IMPLEMENTATION (token/component unification, which happens free at build) so good work is never accidentally ruined — *"do we need to retroactively look at previous work… or will that just ruin good work done and agreed on?"*

5. **Promote the recurring fix into the SYSTEM, not the frame.** When a class of bug (truncation, layout-shift, em-dashes, cramped chips) showed up twice, the fix was a system guarantee, not another hand-patch. The founder demanded exactly this: *"can that be the design floor moving forward?… we should systemize as much as possible instead of just fixing one frame at a time."* That created `ctrl-ds.css` (the floor) + the styleguide, and later the per-component CONTENT INVENTORY ("no matter what populates components… an inventory of all the different possibilities").

6. **Test the worst case, never the happy path.** The recurring failure was mocks pixel-tuned to ONE demo string that shattered on real generated text. The founder pushed past it to the endgame: *"at the end we're going to have to figure out how to make all of this bulletproof. No matter what populates components."* That produced the stress-gallery (100 numbered worst-case cells) as the fixture matrix — the proof, not the hope.

7. **Numbered cells make remote, async review frictionless.** When live co-review was impractical, the founder asked: *"can you number each one so I can refer to the number when giving feedback? I look and voice note whilst looking."* Numbering each fixture turned a vague "this looks off" into precise, addressable feedback (*"recap #12-27 ugly/cut-off… chips&tiers #28-41 badges too small"*) — a cheap mechanic that 10x'd the signal-to-noise of each pass.

8. **Commit-blind reactions beat anchored ones.** The founder reacted to the mock before being told the rationale, which surfaced honest gut responses (*"the desktop decision map is so mundane. I cant imagine anyone using this"*; *"frame 2 just looks like a load of boring waffle I couldnt care less about"*). Unfiltered first-impression reactions to a fresh render caught failures that a guided walk-through would have rationalised away. (The same anti-anchoring instinct became a product law: commit-first stance before CTRL's read.)

9. **Catch the philosophy drift in the words, keep the architecture.** The founder repeatedly separated a sound structure from wrong-stanced copy: *"The example on your latest mock-up feels like it's giving very decisive advice about switching to a free open model… I don't want this app to move towards being blamed for the wrong decision."* The architecture stayed; only the stance of the words was corrected (verdict → map-of-considerations). Diagnosing at the right altitude — copy vs structure — avoided throwing away good work over a fixable surface fault.

10. **Stop at "is this still simple / still honest?" at every step.** The founder governed altitude constantly: *"I honestly think this is way too complicated for mobile… way too much dynamic text"* → the haptic/visual-first reframe; *"I'm a little bit hesitant to go too deep into recommendations"* → clarify-never-recommend. The mock made over-reach visible, and a one-line reaction re-anchored the whole product to its principles before the next surface inherited the drift.

11. **Real-world events as live test fixtures.** The founder fed in a genuine breaking event mid-session (*"just loaded reaction to a Linkedin post by anthropic one hour ago disagreeing with the US governments decision to ban Fable and Mythos… how could we get breaking news like this processed with urgency and treated like a special event?"*). Reacting to a real, unplanned event — not a tidy demo — stress-tested the design against reality and spawned a whole new capability (the special-event fast lane).

12. **The same render-not-description discipline governs the SHIP, not just the mock (added 2026-06-17).** The mock-driven loop reacts to a rendered artifact; the *ship* must be held to the identical bar, where "live" means a prod screenshot of the real surface, never a claim and never the founder's cache. The redesign was once called "live" while prod was still the old UI, and deflecting onto the founder's browser cache broke trust; the relaunch (PR #186, prod-verified with real screenshots) restored it. Same lesson as observation 2, one altitude up: the words about a thing are not the thing, whether it is a mock or a deploy. Treat the founder's "it's still old" as ground truth and go read prod yourself. (See [[feedback_verify_visually_never_deflect]].)

**The meta-lesson:** the mock-driven loop worked because each turn was small, rendered, reacted-to-blind, locked, and (when a fault recurred) promoted into a system guarantee — so the design got monotonically sharper and nothing agreed was ever re-litigated. The founder enjoyed it and asked us to reuse it: *"this could be a really fun way of building the entire app together."*
