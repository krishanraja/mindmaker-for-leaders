## 1. CORE THESES

### A. AI literacy precedes AI strategy. (The slide he'd "take a bullet for.")
> "AI literacy precedes AI strategy. Every leader I know doing real AI strategy at their company has built something with their own hands first. Not a research deck. Not a vendor demo. A prototype." (Slide 7)
> "The reason most leadership teams are tying themselves in knots on AI policy is they're trying to write the policy before anyone in the room has used the tools. The order is: use it, form a literate opinion, then write the policy. Anything else is performance." (notes 2/6/7)
> "The day[s] of getting the guy in the office who 'is good at all this tech stuff' to do it for you are over."

### B. "Non-technical" stopped being a safe identity for a leader.
> "'Non-technical' just stopped being a safe identity for a leader." (Slide 8)
> "In 2023, not being able to build was normal. In 2026, the leaders pulling ahead aren't the most technical people in the room. They're the ones who can describe a problem precisely enough that working software appears, and put it in front of a customer or an engineer the same afternoon."
> "This isn't about learning to code. It's about knowing what's *buildable*, so you stop handing vague requirements to a team and waiting three weeks to find out they built the wrong thing."

### C. Code was never your bottleneck. (The "mindset flip" / the hardest idea to absorb.)
> "Code was never your bottleneck. The scarce thing was never typing the code. It was knowing *what to build*, knowing *what not to build*, and being able to tell the difference. That is a business skill. And you already have more of it than your engineers do, because you live closest to the problem." (Slide 12)
> "Vibe coding didn't make code cheaper to think about. It made code cheaper to *produce*, the part that was already commoditised." → "So the part you're good at just became the scarce, valuable part."

### D. Vibe coding is an EXECUTION, not a conversation. (The single biggest mental-model shift.)
> "Vibe coding is an *execution*, not a conversation." (Slide 14)
> Chatting with an AI is forgiving — "you explore, correct yourself mid-stream, say 'actually, ignore that', and it adapts. The meandering *is* the method."
> Briefing a build is "A brief to a fast, confident contractor. What you say *at the start* substantially decides the quality you get *at the end*."
> "That one mental-model shift, from 'chatting' to 'directing a one-pass execution', is the biggest difference between leaders who ship useful things and leaders who generate expensive messes."

### E. Outputs matter, so inputs matter more. The brief is the new codebase.
> "A partial brief doesn't get you a partial result. It gets you a *mess you pay for twice*." (Slide 23)
> "Small and complete beats large and incomplete, every time. You can't elegantly fix bad inputs after the fact; you can only pay twice."
> "The brief is the *new codebase*." (Slide 25) "Your prompts aren't 'prompt engineering.' They're requirements writing, a skill you already have."
> "Every blank becomes a bug. Every assumption becomes a hallucination. Every vague verb becomes an outage."
> "The vibe is downstream of the brief." (Slide 26)

### F. A prototype is a specification, not a product. (The single most underused move.)
> "A prototype is a *specification*, not a product." (Slide 44)
> "Treat the vibe-coded version as the spec, hand it off to be rebuilt properly, and you've collapsed weeks of ambiguity into an afternoon."
> "The PRD isn't dead. It just *comes later*." (Slide 46) — order flips from "idea → long PRD → debate → build" to "idea → prototype → people experience it → *then* the PRD."

### G. The person with the working demo wins the room. (Cultural, not tooling, shift.)
> "The person with the *working demo* wins the room." (Slide 17)
> "Ideas stop dying in committee. A junior PM with a prototype can move a decision a VP's memo couldn't."
> "When proof gets cheap, the politics of who gets to be right changes."

### H. Division of labour: AI produces, humans take responsibility. (Where he lands.)
> "AI will outpace human coders. Humans stay responsible for what's *riding on it*." (Slide 48)
> "So this isn't 'humans vs AI, who's better.' It's a division of labour: AI produces, humans take responsibility. Get that line right and the sceptic-vs-believer fight just dissolves."
> "AI takes execution. You keep the rest." (Slide 58) — Strategy, Judgment, Distribution, Responsibility.

### I. The power is real; so is the blast radius. Safety is a dial you turn down as stakes rise.
> "The power is real. So is the *blast radius*." (Slide 18)
> "Vibe coding isn't one approach you apply uniformly. It's a dial you turn down as the stakes go up." (Slide 49)
> "Vibe coding is for possibility; engineering is for responsibility." (Slide 50)
> "Treat every AI-generated artifact as untrusted until tested, scanned, and reviewed, at the tier the work demands. That single habit is the whole game." (Slide 56)

### J. Build the capability, don't rent the IP. (The strategic choice.)
> Three honest options (Slide 62): "JUST WANT LEVERAGE TODAY — Use Claude or ChatGPT as a tool." / "WANT A POINT SOLUTION — Great vertical SaaS exists, so buy it, knowing you're renting the IP rather than owning it." / "WANT TO BUILD THE CAPABILITY — Where every week makes the system smarter and the IP stays yours; that's the path I just showed you."

### K. The market already repriced this ($285B / SaaSpocalypse).
> "$285B wiped off software valuations in a single month." (Slide 5) "if a non-developer can build custom software in an afternoon by describing it, the premium for rigid, off-the-shelf SaaS starts to evaporate."
> "The mainstream already arrived; the only question is whether your org noticed." (Slide 15)

## 2. FRAMEWORKS (every named model, sequence, matrix, list)

### Framework 1 — The Staircase (the four waves of who-gets-to-build) — Slide 11
Ordered ascent, "every wave widened who gets to build. This one blows the doors off."
1. **CODE** — CS degree + years. 3–6 months to an MVP. ~30M builders worldwide.
2. **NO-CODE** — Drag-and-drop. 1–2 weeks to an MVP. ~100M builders.
3. **VIBE** — Plain English. Minutes to hours. ~500M potential builders.
4. **AGENTIC** — Intent + review. Minutes, with quality. 1B+ projected.
Strategic punchline: "every business problem that used to need a developer ticket can now be solved by the person who understands the problem most deeply: which is usually not the developer. It's you."

### Framework 2 — Vibe coding → Agentic engineering (entry point vs destination) — Slide 10
- Origin: Andrej Karpathy named it in a Feb 2025 tweet — "I just describe stuff, stuff happens, mostly it works." Collins Dictionary Word of the Year 2025.
- "Vibe coding is the entry point. Agentic engineering, where you set architecture and judgment while AI plans, executes, tests, and iterates, is the destination."

### Framework 3 — Mental Model #1: Architect & Contractor (at 100x speed) — Slide 13
"You are the architect. AI is the contractor, and it works at 100x speed."
**Your job (it can't do this):**
- Know *what* to build
- Define the problem with precision
- Evaluate the output against the business outcome
- Set the guardrails for what must never break
- Read the delta between what you got and what you need
**The catch:** "A contractor at 100x speed will build *whatever* you describe, including something structurally unsound, and it'll cut corners on the fire safety you didn't ask for."

### Framework 4 — Mental Model #2: The Five-Component Brief ("the brief is the new codebase") — Slide 25
"Five components turn a wish into a build":
1. **Goal** — What outcome, for which specific user?
2. **Context** — What systems, data, constraints apply?
3. **Format** — What does the output look like?
4. **Constraints** — What must it never do or touch?
5. **Acceptance** — How will I know it works?
Law: "Every blank becomes a bug. Every assumption becomes a hallucination. Every vague verb becomes an outage."

### Framework 5 — The One Pass vs. The Half-Brief — Slide 23
The speaker-briefing parable: 16-of-20 submitted creates *two* problems — re-doing the first 16 against just-articulated standards + adding 4 while the model has drifted. The fix: "Wait until all 20 are in, set every standard, and submit one complete, fully specified brief once." Scales to "dashboards, CRMs, app features, data scripts."

### Framework 6 — Context Drift (the named failure mode) — Slide 24
- *What it is:* the context window fills, earliest instructions/constraints get pushed out and forgotten, "so it starts contradicting choices it made three tasks ago."
- *Why it sneaks up:* the window holds your prompts AND replies, file contents, error messages, every failed retry. "Looping on a broken fix is the fastest way to burn it."
- *The rule:* "Start fresh the moment you catch yourself saying 'no, I already told you that.'"

### Framework 7 — Context Engineering: The Operations Manual in Four Files — Slide 28
"The gap between amateur and expert isn't prompts. It's the *context infrastructure* you build around the work." Build the manual in four files:
1. **The Project Constitution (Rules file)** — "Loads into every session: what the tool does, the architecture already decided, the rules that must never break, your conventions." Skip it and the model defaults to its training averages, not your stack.
2. **What Has Been Built (Architecture files)** — "The entities and how they relate, what's connected, the field names that matter, what's done / in progress / next. Several focused files beat one giant document."
3. **Reusable Know-How (Skills files)** — "Prompt components for what you do repeatedly: your reporting standards, a CRM-integration pattern, a pre-deploy security checklist. Chain in only the ones a task needs."
4. **Visual Consistency (A design system)** — "One page: colours, type, 'always use these components, never invent new patterns.'"
Purpose: "That is how *your taste*: not the model's averages, ends up in the output."

### Framework 8 — The Macro Arc: The Shape of Every Good Build (5 steps, in order) — Slide 30
1. **Research** 2. **Spec** 3. **Blueprint** 4. **Small units** 5. **Validate**
"Spec the what before opening any tool. Let an AI interview you, 15–20 questions, one at a time, until the problem is clearer than you've ever needed it to be." "Most of the work is upstream of the tool." Blueprint breaks the build into small units, "each with an acceptance test, so 'done' is defined before a line is written."

### Framework 9 — The Four Session Habits (clean build vs spaghetti) — Slide 31
1. **Plan mode, then execute mode** — "Never explore and execute in the same thread."
2. **One task, one conversation** — "The moment you hear yourself say 'also… while you're at it…', start a new session."
3. **Build in tiny, complete slices** — "One endpoint, one view, one flow, fully briefed, then ship and test it before stacking the next. The discipline lives in the boundary."
4. **Ask "why is this breaking?"** — "Don't say 'fix this bug'; make the AI diagnose the root cause before it patches. Patches-on-patches is how technical debt quietly accelerates."
"None of this is technical. It's the operational discipline of a good manager, applied to a contractor that works at a hundred times speed."

### Framework 10 — The Tool Taxonomy: Four Tiers by Autonomy — Slide 33
Ordered by "autonomy over your codebase and terminal":
1. **Component generators** — Beautiful UI components in seconds. *v0.* (LOW autonomy)
2. **Full-stack sandboxes** — Browser, app, deployed. *Lovable, Bolt, Replit.* (MEDIUM)
3. **AI-native IDEs** — Indexes your whole codebase. *Cursor, Windsurf.* (HIGH)
4. **Autonomous CLI agents** — Walk away. Repo + API key + task. *Claude Code, Codex, Aider.* (TOTAL)
"As autonomy goes up, the brief has to get tighter — because you're reviewing less of the work."

### Framework 11 — Which Tool For Which Job: Three Rungs (the practical map) — Slide 34
1. **App builders (START HERE · non-technical)** — Lovable, Bolt, Base44, Replit. "This is where most of you should begin."
2. **Polished front-ends (more control)** — v0 by Vercel. "Beautiful, production-quality UI components for stakeholder demos and real interfaces."
3. **Code editors & agents (power users)** — Cursor, Claude Code. "Real architecture and real repos, where prototypes become products." (Cursor: $2B ARR, 70% of the Fortune 1000.)
The path: "start in Lovable or Bolt → sync to GitHub → graduate to Cursor for anything production-critical. You blend tools or match them to your needs; you don't marry one."

### Framework 12 — The Two Commercial Moves ("why it's worth your weekend") — Slides 36/61
- **MOVE 01:** "Arm your people with the right information at the moment of action. The dashboard your CRM never gave you, built around how your team actually sells, not how a vendor decided everyone should."
- **MOVE 02:** "Stop selling what's built. Build what your customers are telling you. Prototype the idea from real feedback instead of pitching an abstraction through ten meetings."

### Framework 13 — The Product-Leadership Flywheel (5 steps) — Slide 46
1. **Capture feedback** 2. **Spot the pattern** 3. **Prototype the core flow** 4. **Validate with 3 customers** 5. **Hand engineering the working spec.**
"5–10× faster than the PRD-first cycle, with far less rework downstream." "Run this loop a dozen times and you've built a direct, high-velocity line between customer need and shipped reality, something no competitor copies by buying a tool."

### Framework 14 — Match the Use Case to the Method (the safety dial, 3 settings) — Slide 49
- ✅ **Full speed** — Internal dashboards, trackers, landing pages, stakeholder demos, with light review.
- ⚠️ **With review** — Client-facing apps, anything touching customer PII, with a security scan and human-led review.
- ⛔ **Human-led** — Financial processing, healthcare, anything compliance-bound, a core system of record, "where AI assists but never owns it."

### Framework 15 — The Three Handoff Questions ("when does it need a professional engineer?") — Slide 50
1. **Who will use it?** Vibe mode: just you/your team. Engineers: external customers log in, transact, or depend on it.
2. **What happens if it breaks?** Vibe mode: a minor annoyance. Engineers: damage to revenue, trust, or anything regulated.
3. **How long must it live?** Vibe mode: one-off / short-lived. Engineers: a core system that runs for years with many hands.
"If two of the three lean high-stakes, that is your handoff moment." Hand off well: "Hand over the working app with your rules file and architecture notes, and ask engineering for a risk assessment rather than a quote."

### Framework 16 — The Safety Ladder ("when do you start caring?", 5 rungs) — Slide 51
1. **Solo Tinkerer** — no data, no users; blast radius is just you. Build anything.
2. **Solopreneur** — first real customer data; backups and secrets start to matter.
3. **Mom & Pop / Team** — others depend on it; needs an owner + a second pair of eyes.
4. **SMB** — revenue rides on it; add review gates, access control, a registry.
5. **Enterprise** — compliance, PII, audits; full governance non-negotiable.
"The mistake isn't building fast; it's not knowing which rung you're standing on. Find yourself on this ladder before you ship."

### Framework 17 — The Six Handoff Signals (project outgrown vibe mode) — Slide 52
1. **Real customers** 2. **Regulated data** (PII, payments, health; GDPR/PCI/HIPAA/SOC 2) 3. **You can't reason about it** ("three patches deep on patches") 4. **It must live for years** 5. **You can't test it** 6. **Real infrastructure** ("If Git feels like magic, get help before going live.")
"Rebuilding is usually faster than retrofitting."

### Framework 18 — The Four Governance Habits ("the minimum that makes speed sustainable") — Slide 56
1. **A registry** — list of every internal tool touching customer/financial/operational data: what it does, what it depends on, who owns it.
2. **An owner** — "Not whoever prompted it, but whoever maintains it, updates the logic, and decommissions it. Without this, tools pile up like browser tabs."
3. **A review gate** — anything past "full speed" gets a human check: secrets out of prompts, access not over-permissive, a security scan.
4. **Data classification** — "Decide before you build: what an external model may see, what stays in-house, what needs encryption."

### Framework 19 — The Action Matrix (2×2: Frequency × Judgment) — Slide 57 ("MIND SET · PHASE 01: Map every workflow before you write a single brief.")
- **HIGH FREQ · HIGH JUDGMENT → Delegate** — "AI drafts. You approve."
- **LOW FREQ · HIGH JUDGMENT → Template** — "Build it once. Reuse with care."
- **HIGH FREQ · LOW JUDGMENT → Automate** — "Pure vibe coding territory."
- **LOW FREQ · LOW JUDGMENT → Kill** — "Do not even build it. Stop doing it."
Note (notes 57): "Most things you're tempted to vibe-code first are actually quadrant 4. Don't."

### Framework 20 — The Four Things You Keep ("the line you leave with") — Slide 58
**Strategy** (what to build and not) · **Judgment** (when it's good enough) · **Distribution** (getting it in front of people who'll pay) · **Responsibility** (owning what's riding on the output).

### Framework 21 — The Leverage-Audit Question (the homework) — Slide 59
> "What did I do this week that I'll do again next week, and could a system do it *eighty percent* as well?"
> "Not the moonshot or the AI strategy deck, just that question. Answer it honestly and you'll find three candidates in five minutes. Repetition is the signal, not how much it annoys you."

### Framework 22 — The Weekend Homework (3 steps) — Slide 60
1. **Pick the workflow** — "scope it down until it almost feels too small."
2. **Write the five-part brief** — Goal, context, format, constraints, acceptance, no blanks. "Let an AI interview you first."
3. **Build it & ship to one person** — Lovable if non-technical, ship to one real user, "treat the result as a spec rather than gospel."
"Do this once and the abstraction breaks for good. You'll never brief a vendor or an engineer the same vague way again."

### Framework 23 — The Three Skills-File Templates (take-home briefs) — Slide 29
- **DASHBOARD BUILD:** Role & job-to-be-done · Primary metrics (source, calc, benchmark) · Data sources (every integration + fields) · Access rules · Acceptance (3–5 testable truths)
- **CUSTOMER-VALIDATED PROTOTYPE:** The feedback (exact customer quotes) · User story (as a… I want… so that…) · Core flow (the only thing that must work) · Not in scope · Success (completes the flow, no instructions)
- **BATCH DOCUMENTS (the briefing fix):** All N inputs (gathered, submitted together) · Uniform standards · Identical output format · Quality check (verify before emitting) · One pass.
"Every input, every standard, and the full output format specified *before a single output is generated*."

### Framework 24 — Five Through-Lines ("if you remember five things") — Slide 61
1. Code was never the bottleneck. 2. It's an execution, not a conversation. 3. Context infrastructure carries your taste. 4. Two moves pay for the weekend. 5. AI produces; you stay responsible.

### Framework 25 — The "False Green" verification principle — Slide 55
"The 'false green': it reports success while doing nothing." "Never let a worker grade its own homework. Verify against the real artifact: the row, the file, the email that did or didn't send, never the worker's claim about it." "A false alarm wastes your time. A false green destroys the work while reassuring you it's thriving."

### Framework 26 — The Build-vs-Buy repricing (Old Way / New Way) — Slide 43
- OLD WAY: **$30k** — agency quote, six-week timeline, one stakeholder review, "one shot at getting the brief right."
- NEW WAY: **<$200/mo** — "The whole stack: builder + editor + agent. You iterate the same afternoon and try again for free when you're wrong."
"The build-vs-buy math didn't shift a little. It shifted by two orders of magnitude."

## 3. LEADER BELIEFS — what an AI-native leader must DO / THINK / FEEL / STOP

### MUST DO
- **Build something with their own hands first** — a prototype, before any AI strategy. Literacy precedes strategy.
- **Write a complete five-component brief** before building; treat the brief as the codebase.
- **Let an AI interview them** (15–20 questions, one at a time) to spec the problem before opening a tool.
- **Maintain context infrastructure** — a constitution, architecture files, skills files, a design system — and chain the right ones per task.
- **Work one task per conversation**; start fresh the moment they say "no, I already told you that" or "also, while you're at it…".
- **Make the AI diagnose before it patches** — "ask 'why is this breaking?'".
- **Run the leverage audit weekly:** "What did I do this week that I'll do again next week, and could a system do it 80% as well?"
- **Verify against the real artifact**, never the worker's claim ("false green").
- **Turn the safety dial** with the stakes; install a registry, an owner, a review gate, data classification.
- **Treat the prototype as the spec** and hand engineering a working, validated thing + rules file + architecture notes, asking for a *risk assessment, not a quote.*
- **Make building "a normal, visible, encouraged part of how the team thinks"** — govern shadow IT instead of banning it.

### MUST THINK
- "Code was never your bottleneck." The scarce skill is knowing what to build and what NOT to build — a business skill the leader already owns by living closest to the problem.
- "The brief is the new codebase" / "the vibe is downstream of the brief."
- "A prototype is a specification, not a product." / "The PRD comes later."
- "Vibe coding is for possibility; engineering is for responsibility."
- "AI produces; you stay responsible." The leader keeps Strategy, Judgment, Distribution, Responsibility.

- "You blend tools… you don't marry one."

### MUST FEEL

- Ownership/agency: "I'm an operator who got sick of waiting for things to be built and started building them." The dependency on "someone else's calendar" should *dissolve*.
- Permission and confidence: "the person with the deepest domain knowledge builds the thing, because they finally can."
- Calm: Mindmaker's promise — "TURNING AI CHAOS INTO CALM, CLEAR, EXECUTABLE DIRECTION."

### MUST STOP DOING
- Stop "handing vague requirements to a team and waiting three weeks to find out they built the wrong thing."
- Stop **chatting** with the AI when you mean to **direct a one-pass execution.**
- Stop submitting half-briefs ("16 of 20") — you only "pay twice."
- Stop looping on a broken fix / patches-on-patches.
- Stop letting "a fast, confident, unreviewed thing become load-bearing without anyone deciding it should."
- Stop letting a worker grade its own homework.
- Stop building quadrant-4 (low-freq, low-judgment) things — "Kill. Do not even build it."
- Stop pitching abstractions through ten meetings; stop "selling what's built."
- Stop relying on "the guy in the office who is good at all this tech stuff."

## 4. PRODUCT IMPLICATIONS for CTRL

CTRL is the decision/leadership product for the exact leader this deck addresses. The deck is, in effect, CTRL's worldview. Concrete implications:

### The app should make the BRIEF effortless and central
- CTRL should treat **"the brief is the new codebase"** as a first-class object. When a leader wants to act on a decision, CTRL should help them produce a *complete five-component brief* (Goal, Context, Format, Constraints, Acceptance) — never a half-brief. Surface "every blank becomes a bug" as live guidance: flag missing components before output.
- Bake in **the AI interview**: CTRL should *interview the leader* (15–20 questions, one at a time) to sharpen a fuzzy intent into a precise spec before producing anything. This is literally CTRL's "Diagnosis Room" instinct — pressure-test the thinking first.

### "The next step" for a leader = a small, complete, shippable slice
- CTRL's "next step" should always be scoped down "until it almost feels too small" — one decision, one flow, one artifact, fully specified, with an explicit acceptance test ("done" defined before work begins). Mirror the Macro Arc (Research → Spec → Blueprint → Small units → Validate) and "build in tiny, complete slices."
- Enforce **one task, one conversation.** When CTRL detects drift / "also, while you're at it…" / "no, I already told you that," it should proactively offer a clean, fresh, context-loaded session rather than letting the thread rot.

### Context infrastructure = what CTRL should LEARN AND REMEMBER about the leader
This is the strongest product directive. CTRL should persist, per leader, the equivalent of the **four-file operations manual**, so their *taste* — not the model's averages — ends up in every output:
1. **Constitution / Rules** — who they are, what they're building, decisions already made, rules that must never break, their conventions.
2. **Architecture / What's been built** — their ventures, entities, the decisions/projects done / in-progress / next, the field-names and metrics that matter to them.
3. **Skills / Reusable know-how** — their repeated moves: reporting standards, briefing patterns, their decision rubrics, security/review checklists — chained in per task.
4. **Design system / Voice** — their tone, their phrasing, how they want things to look and read — so consistency compounds instead of drifting.
CTRL "chains the right combination per task." The longer a leader uses CTRL, the smarter the system gets and the more it speaks/decides in their grain — directly echoing "every week makes the system smarter and the IP stays yours."

### Make the safety dial automatic
- CTRL should classify each decision/build by stakes and apply **the dial** (✅ full speed / ⚠️ with review / ⛔ human-led) and the **three handoff questions** (who uses it, what if it breaks, how long it lives). When two of three lean high-stakes, CTRL flags the handoff moment.
- Surface a lightweight **registry + owner + review gate + data classification** for anything the leader builds or decides that becomes load-bearing — so speed stays sustainable.
- Verification by default ("false green"): CTRL must verify against the real artifact, never a worker's/agent's claim of success. Anything CTRL automates should be checked against the row/file/email that did-or-didn't happen. (Strongly resonant with the user's own ops scars — "false green," "never grade its own homework.")

### Triage like the Action Matrix
- CTRL should help the leader sort their recurring work into Delegate / Template / Automate / Kill (Frequency × Judgment), and aggressively recommend **Kill** for low-freq/low-judgment items. The weekly leverage-audit question ("what did I do this week I'll do again, that a system could do 80% as well?") is a natural recurring CTRL ritual that yields "three candidates in five minutes."

### Decisions/prototypes are specs, not gospel
- When CTRL produces a recommendation or artifact, frame it as **"evidence of what's needed,"** a spec to validate with real people — not a finished product. Support the Product-Leadership Flywheel: capture feedback → spot the pattern → prototype the core flow → validate with 3 → hand off the working spec.

### Tone of the product
- "Turning AI chaos into calm, clear, executable direction." CTRL should reduce a leader's chaos to *one clear next move* — radical simplicity, one-step-per-state, guided/sequenced (consistent with the user's CTRL UX principles in memory). The deck's repeated "let it land," "pause," single-idea-per-slide rhythm maps onto CTRL's minimalist, one-decision-at-a-time surface.

### The journey CTRL embodies
"Free → blueprint → built." CTRL helps the leader **make their mind up** ("Mindmaker is named for a reason… I'm here to help you make your mind up"), then move — "This isn't a coding race. It's deciding what shape you're becoming."
